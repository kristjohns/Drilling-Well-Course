## 01:16  01 The system and the rules

**[01:19]** Let's start with the hardware. At the top, the wellhead connector of the blowout preventer latches onto the high pressure wellhead housing. The top of that housing is the wellhead datum, the reference point for every load in this method. The housing is welded to a wellhead extension, which continues down as the 20 inch surface casing. Around it sits the low pressure conductor housing, welded to a conductor extension and the 36 inch conductor. The conductor housing lands in a well slot of the template, a steel frame on the seabed. Below the mudline the conductor is cemented into the soil, and the surface casing is cemented inside the conductor.  
**[02:06]** The standard defines the conductor as the tubulars installed and cemented in a well, providing support for, and load sharing with, the surface casing. Notice the words load sharing. Most of this tutorial is really about who carries what.  
**[02:25]** The rules are spread over several parts of the standard. Clause 8.1 says that the marine drilling riser loads in Annex A are the riser forces for the whole wellhead system: wellhead housing, wellhead extension, conductor housing and conductor extension, and that the load share between wellhead system and template shall be determined. Clause 13.3.6 covers the structural design of the template, the conductor and the load share. Annex A gives us the fatigue loads. Annex B gives the design format and the fatigue capacity. And Annex J describes the load paths through the well. Two documents outside the standard do the rest: DNV-RP-C203 for the S-N curves, and NORSOK N-001 for the template's design fatigue factors.  
**[03:23]** Here is the whole method as a recipe of eight steps. One, get the fatigue load at the wellhead datum. Two, follow the load paths and find the load share. Three, build a local response model of the well. Four, convert moments into hot spot stresses. Five, turn S-N curves into M-N curves. Six, sum the damage. Seven, apply the design factor. And eight, screen, test the sensitivities, and mitigate. Before we can do any of that, we need one idea in our heads. What exactly is fatigue?  

## 04:02  02 Fatigue and the S-N curve

**[04:06]** The standard defines fatigue as a process of progressive, localized structural change, under fluctuating stresses, that can end in cracks or complete fracture after enough load cycles. In a welded steel structure the story almost always starts at a weld toe. The weld leaves a sharp notch, tiny flaws and residual stress. Each cycle opens a microscopic crack a tiny bit more. Millions of cycles later, the crack is through the wall.  
**[04:39]** Let's name the parts of a stress cycle. The stress range, Δσ, is the maximum minus the minimum. The amplitude is half of that, and the mean sits in the middle. The ratio of minimum to maximum is called R. NORSOK asks for fully reversed loading, R equal to minus one, so the cycle swings symmetrically about zero.  
**[05:05]** Why do we only care about the range, and not the mean? Because a fresh weld carries residual tensile stress close to yield. Whatever the nominal mean is, the material right at the toe is cycling down from that high tensile level. Two cycles with the same range do the same damage. That is why every S-N curve for welded joints is written in terms of the stress range alone.  
**[05:35]** Now, where does the S-N curve come from? Let's derive it. A fatigue crack grows a small amount every cycle. Experiments show that the growth per cycle, d a by d N, is proportional to the stress intensity range, delta K, raised to a power m. This is the Paris law. The stress intensity range is the stress range, times the square root of pi times the crack depth, times a geometry factor Y.  
**[06:06]** Substitute delta K into the Paris law and separate the variables: d N equals d a, divided by C, times Y to the m, times Δσ to the m, times pi a to the power m over two. Now integrate from the initial flaw size to the final crack size. The integral depends only on the geometry and the flaw sizes, not on the load. So it is just a constant. And what is left is beautifully simple. The number of cycles, times the stress range to the power m, is a constant. We call that constant a bar.  
**[06:49]** Take the logarithm of both sides and you get log N equals log a bar, minus m times log Δσ. On log log paper that is a straight line. The slope m is the inverse slope of the S-N curve, and for welded steel it is about three, the same exponent the Paris law gives for steel. A doubling of the stress range divides the life by two to the power three. Eight times shorter.  
**[07:22]** Real test data scatter. At a given stress range, log N follows roughly a normal distribution. The design S-N curve is placed two standard deviations below the mean. As NORSOK puts it, 2.3 % of the test values are expected to fall below the curve. A 97.7 % probability of survival.  
**[07:48]** NORSOK sends us to DNV-RP-C203 for the curves themselves. They come in families. In air, the curve bends at 10 million cycles, from slope three to a flatter slope five. In seawater with cathodic protection, the bend comes earlier, at 1 million cycles. And under free corrosion there is no bend at all. We will use the C1 curve in seawater with cathodic protection. For fewer than a million cycles, m one is three and log a bar is 12.049. Above a million cycles, m two is five and log a bar is 16.081. These are the values from the 2016 edition, for teaching. The 2024 edition that NORSOK refers to revised the seawater curves, so always take design values from the edition in your design basis.  
**[08:52]** One more correction. Thick sections are weaker in fatigue than thin ones, because the stress at the weld toe decays more slowly through the thickness and there is more volume for flaws. The S-N curve handles this with a thickness factor: the stress range is multiplied by t over t ref, to the power k, with t ref equal to 25 millimetres. For our 2 inch conductor wall and k equal to 0.15, the stress is scaled up by about 11 %.  
**[09:30]** And here is a direct requirement from the standard. Girth welds on the conductor, for example the weld between the conductor housing and the conductor extension, shall meet S-N class C1. C1 sits above the classes you would give an ordinary as-welded joint, so the weld profile, the fit-up tolerances and the inspection have to be good enough to justify it.  

## 09:57  03 From waves to the wellhead moment

**[10:00]** Step one of the method is the fatigue load. Follow the chain. Waves move the rig. The rig drags the top of the riser around. The riser rotates in the lower flex joint just above the stack, and the tall, heavy BOP stack sways on the wellhead like an inverted pendulum. All of that arrives at the wellhead datum as a bending moment and a shear force.  
**[10:29]** Let's derive the relation between the moment and the shear. Treat the lower flex joint as a hinge that passes a horizontal force Q but almost no moment. It sits a height H above the wellhead datum. Take moments about the datum, and the moment there is simply Q times H. So the shear at the datum is the moment divided by H. That is exactly the formula in the footnotes to tables A four and A five. For the standard design case, H is up to 13.7 metres. With the BOP on top of a christmas tree, the lever grows by the height of the tree, up to another 2.5 metres.  
**[11:18]** Now cut the well at a depth z below the datum. The same force Q now has a longer lever, H plus z. So the moment is Mw times one plus z over H. This is why the standard says that the moment below the datum shall be increased due to the shear force. In our example the mudline is 4 metres below the datum, so the moment there is already 29 % bigger than at the datum.  
**[11:51]** Annex A gives two kinds of riser load. The extreme and accidental moments in tables A four and A five are for static design. For fatigue, the standard specifies a single load bending moment range, and a general bending moment histogram. Let's look at both.  
**[12:12]** Table A.7 gives the annual single load bending moment range, ΔMo, at the wellhead datum. It depends on the water depth, and on whether the BOP sits on the wellhead or on a tree. Notice that shallow water is the worst case: a short, stiff riser passes more of the vessel motion down to the well. And the tree mode is always worse than the wellhead mode, because the stack is taller and heavier.  
**[12:44]** Table A.8 is the shape of the year. It lists normalised moment ranges from 0.1 to 2.9, and how many cycles of each happen in one year. Small cycles are extremely common: almost 4 million cycles at 0.1. The biggest cycles happen once or twice a year. In total that is 7.47 million cycles per year, roughly one every four seconds.  
**[13:15]** To get the histogram for a real project, you multiply every range in table A.8 by the single load range from table A.7. The standard's own example: for 80 to 250 metres of water, 0.1 times 2124 gives 212 kilonewton metres, 3.9 million times a year. Our example well is in 350 metres of water with the BOP on the wellhead, so ΔMo is 1436 kilonewton metres, and the bins run from about 140, up to over 4000 kilonewton metres.  
**[13:58]** Two more rules from Annex A. The fatigue service life is the fatigue life divided by the design factor, and it is equivalent to the permissible number of operational days with a BOP and riser connected. For both the template and the wellhead system, the minimum calculated fatigue service life is one year. And the histogram does not include vortex induced vibration of the riser. If that is relevant, its damage must be added.  

## 14:30  04 Load paths and load share

**[14:33]** Step two. Where does the moment go once it enters the well? Annex J answers with eight contact areas. F, where the connector meets the wellhead. A and B, where the wellhead housing bears against the conductor housing, at the top and at the bottom. C and D, where the conductor housing bears against the template slot. G, where the template sits on the seabed. E, the top of the cement between conductor and surface casing. And H, the conductor against the soil and cement.  
**[15:09]** Through those contacts the load can travel along three paths. Path one goes down the wellhead extension and the surface casing, and into the soil below the top of the cement. Path two jumps across to the conductor housing at A and B, and goes down the conductor into the soil. Path three goes from the conductor housing into the template at C and D, and through the template into the seabed. The standard says the load sharing depends on tolerances and on the relative stiffness of all the elements, including the soil. So let's understand stiffness.  
**[15:51]** Picture two springs side by side under a rigid bar. Push the bar down with a force F. Both springs must move by the same amount, delta. That is compatibility. Each spring pushes back with its stiffness times delta. That is the material law. And the two forces must add up to F. That is equilibrium. Solve for delta, put it back, and the share of each spring is its stiffness divided by the total stiffness. Stiffness attracts load. That one line is the core of every load share analysis.  
**[16:32]** Vertically, the well and the template's foundation are two springs in parallel. Table 4 gives indicative shares carried by the structure: from 50 % for a single caisson down to 20 % for the centre slot of a six slot template. Under the fully supporting conductor methodology, which is the main principle, the conductor is designed to carry all vertical hang-off loads, while the template is still designed for its stiffness based share. Each element is designed for the share that is worst for it.  
**[17:08]** The same idea works for bending. Below the top of the cement, the conductor and the surface casing are glued together, so they must bend to the same curvature, kappa. Each string resists with its own bending stiffness, EI, times kappa, and the moments add up. So each string takes its EI divided by the total EI. For a 36 by 2 inch conductor around a 20 by 1 inch casing, the conductor takes about 92 %. But above the top of the cement, in the cement shortfall, the strings are not bonded. The surface casing must carry its share on its own, which is why the standard calls cement shortfall one of the main factors in wellhead fatigue.  
**[17:59]** Now the conductor template interface. The slot holds the conductor housing at two rings, C and D, a distance h apart. If the housing tilts by a small angle theta, one ring is pushed by theta times h over two, the other the opposite way. The two ring forces form a couple, and the couple divided by theta is a rotational stiffness: k times h squared over two. With different ring stiffnesses, it becomes the product over the sum, times h squared. For rings of 200 and 400 meganewtons per metre, 2 metres apart, that is about 530 meganewton metres per radian. The template and the well below it are two rotational springs in parallel, and the moment splits by the same stiffness rule. But real slots have a radial clearance. Until the gap closes, the template carries nothing at all. So the share depends on the size of the cycle, and that makes the problem nonlinear.  
**[19:08]** Vertically, the interface has a different job. A producing well heats up and grows. The free growth of a steel string is the expansion coefficient, times the temperature rise, times the free length. 12 millionths per degree, times 60 degrees, times 120 metres, is about 86 millimetres. If you stopped that growth completely, the force would be E A alpha delta T, almost 6 meganewtons in the surface casing alone. So the standard requires the template design to accommodate a relative vertical displacement of 100 millimetres between template and conductor, including thermal growth and settlement. The slot must let the well slide vertically, and still hold it laterally.  

## 19:59  05 The conductor in the soil

**[20:02]** Below the mudline, the conductor is a beam pushed against the soil. The simplest model treats the soil as a bed of independent springs: the soil pushes back with a force per metre equal to a modulus k times the lateral deflection y. Take a short slice of the conductor. The change in shear force along the slice equals the soil reaction. The change in moment equals the shear. And the moment is EI times the curvature. Put the three together and you get EI times the fourth derivative of y, plus k times y, equals zero.  
**[20:44]** Try a solution of the form e to the lambda z. The equation becomes EI lambda to the fourth plus k equals zero. If we define beta as the fourth root of k over four EI, the four roots are beta times plus or minus one, plus or minus i. Far down the conductor the deflection has to die out, so we keep only the two decaying roots: an exponential decay times a cosine and a sine. One over beta is a length, the distance over which the conductor feels the soil.  
**[21:23]** At the mudline, the stick-up hands over a moment M zero and a shear P. Differentiate the solution twice and three times, set the moment and the shear at the mudline, and the two constants fall out. The moment down the conductor is M zero times e to the minus beta x, times cosine plus sine, plus P over beta, times e to the minus beta x, times sine. A decaying, slightly oscillating wave of bending.  
**[21:56]** Where is the moment largest? Set the derivative to zero, and the tangent of beta x equals P, divided by two beta M zero plus P. For our conductor in soil with k equal to 5 megapascals, one over beta is 6.8 metres, and the peak is 1.33 times the wellhead moment, about 1 metre below the mudline. In softer soil the peak moves deeper, and the bending reaches further down. The same solution also tells us how stiff the well is in rotation at the mudline: EI times beta, about 390 meganewton metres per radian. Compare that with the template's 530, and in this simple picture the template would take more than half of the moment, if there were no gap.  
**[22:51]** Real soil is less tidy. Its resistance is nonlinear, grows with depth and softens under cycling. And the interface depends on how the conductor went in. Driven gives the best contact, while a drilled and cemented conductor is the least predictable, with the poorest horizontal support. That is why NORSOK does not rely on one hand calculation. It asks for a local response model, and for the fatigue capacity to be determined over a range of parameters.  

## 23:25  06 From moment to hot-spot stress

**[23:28]** Step four turns moments into stresses. Start with beam theory. Plane sections stay plane, so the strain grows linearly with the distance y from the neutral axis: strain equals curvature times y. Steel is elastic, so stress is E times strain. Add up the moment of all those stresses over the cross section and you get E times kappa times the integral of y squared, which is EI kappa. Eliminate kappa, and the stress is M y over I. At the outer surface, it is M divided by the section modulus W.  
**[24:09]** For a tube, the trick is to integrate in rings. The polar moment, the integral of r squared over the area, is pi over two times r outer to the fourth minus r inner to the fourth. By symmetry, the bending moment of inertia is half of that. In diameters: pi over 64, times D to the fourth minus d to the fourth. For our conductor, 914 millimetres outside and 813 inside, I is 1.29 times 10¹⁰ millimetres to the fourth, and W is 2.82 times 10⁷ cubic millimetres.  
**[24:54]** Here is our first hot spot, GW1, the girth weld between the conductor housing and the conductor extension, 3 metres below the datum. When the wellhead sees a moment range of 3000 kilonewton metres, the shear lever pushes the moment up on the way down, and the load share to the template and the casing pulls it back. The local model says the conductor carries 2583 kilonewton metres at this weld. Divide by W, and the nominal stress range is 91.6 megapascals.  
**[25:33]** The weld itself raises the stress. The most important reason in a girth weld is misalignment. Offset two plates by e and pull them with a force N: the line of force has to jog sideways, which creates a moment N times e at the weld. If both sides restrain it equally, each takes half, and the extra bending stress is three N e over t squared. So the stress concentration factor is one plus three e over t. A tube restrains the offset better than a flat plate, and DNV gives the tubular version. In our example we use an SCF of 1.10, so the hot spot stress range is 100.7 megapascals.  
**[26:24]** Our second hot spot, TS1, is in the template: the weld where the upper slot ring meets its support beams. The ring force is shared by two beams, each fixed at both ends and loaded at mid span. A fixed-fixed beam with a central load P has a mid span moment of P L over eight, so each beam sees F L over 16. With an SCF of two, that gives 0.156 megapascals per kilonewton of ring force. At a wellhead moment range of 3000, the ring force range is 598 kilonewtons, and the hot spot stress range is 93 megapascals.  
**[27:12]** In a real assessment, the critical regions are found by finite element analysis, and the standard notes that four to six hot spots with the lowest capacity are usually enough. Typical candidates are the wellhead housing to extension weld, the surface casing near the top of the cement, the conductor girth welds, and the template slot welds. We follow two of them all the way: GW1 and TS1.  

## 27:41  07 Building the M-N curve

**[27:45]** Step five. The load histogram is written in wellhead moments, but the S-N curve is written in stress. So NORSOK asks us to express the capacity directly in moments: an M-N curve, the number of cycles to failure against the moment range at the wellhead datum. Load and capacity then live at the same point, and comparing them becomes trivial.  
**[28:11]** If the stress were simply proportional to the moment, Δσ equal to kappa times ΔM, the conversion would be one line. Put it into the S-N curve: N equals a bar sigma, times kappa ΔM, to the minus m. Group the constants, and you get the same power law in the moment, with a new intercept, a bar sigma times kappa to the minus m. That is formula B.8, and in logarithms, B.9. The slope does not change. Only the intercept moves.  
**[28:50]** But in a wellhead, the stress is not proportional to the moment. Contacts open and close, gaps take up, and preload is overcome. Look at our girth weld: the curve bends over as the template slot starts to help. And the template weld sees nothing at all until the 2 millimetre gap closes. That is why the standard requires finite element analysis to find the stress ranges.  
**[29:19]** The procedure in clause B.4.2.4 goes like this. A, build a three dimensional elastic model with all the contacts, preloads, cement shortfall, friction, tolerances and clearances. B, apply a moment at the datum, together with its shear, the moment over H. C, apply it plus, then minus, fully reversed, and compute the stress range at every hot spot, iterating until the contacts converge. D, read the cycles to failure from the S-N curve. E, report those cycles against the moment range. And f, repeat with different moment levels until you cover 10⁴ to 10⁸ cycles. The standard suggests six levels, halving each time, from 6000 down to 187.5 kilonewton metres.  
**[30:16]** For this tutorial we built a simplified version of that model, two beam strings, the wellhead and casing inside, the conductor housing and conductor outside, joined at the contacts, supported by the template rings with a 2 millimetre gap, glued together below the top of the cement, and resting on soil springs. It is a teaching stand-in for the three dimensional model, but it follows the same steps, so let's run them.  
**[30:46]** Six moment ranges, plus and minus each. At 6000 kilonewton metres the girth weld sees 200 megapascals and fails after about 100 000 cycles. At 3000, a hundred megapascals, and about 800 000 cycles. At the low end, the stresses are a few megapascals and the lives run into the hundreds of billions of cycles. The template weld follows the same pattern, but it falls away much faster at small moments, because there the gap is open.  
**[31:23]** Because the S-N curve has two slopes, the natural capacity format is a bilinear M-N curve, formula B.10: slope m one for large moments, slope m two for small ones. Where do the branches meet? Set the two expressions for log N equal. The log of the moment at the knee is the difference of the intercepts, divided by the difference of the slopes. That is formula B.11.  
**[31:53]** The standard asks for a lower bound, a capacity curve at or below every point. A simple way is to keep the S-N slopes, three and five, and slide each branch down until no point lies below it. In logs, each intercept is the smallest value of log N plus m log ΔM over the points of that branch. For GW1 that gives 16.33 for the steep branch, 22.75 for the flat branch, and a knee at about 1600 kilonewton metres. Notice that the flat branch is set by the smallest moment, where the gap is open and the conductor works alone. The lower bound protects you from exactly that kind of nonlinearity.  
**[32:45]** Two more items complete the capacity. The M-N curve shall include a fatigue life reduction factor, RF, from DNV-RP-C203 section 5.7. It divides the cycles, so it simply lowers log a bar by log RF. We keep it at one in this example so the arithmetic stays visible. Just remember that the life scales with one over RF. And the standard asks you to tabulate ΔMc, the single load fatigue capacity at 1 million cycles. For our girth weld it is about 2780 kilonewton metres, and for the template weld about 2840.  

## 33:35  08 Damage, life and the design check

**[33:39]** Step six is the damage. If a moment range gives failure after N i cycles, then one cycle uses up a fraction, one over N i, of the life. N i cycles use up n i over N i. The Palmgren Miner rule simply adds these fractions over all the bins, ignoring the order in which the cycles come. Failure is predicted when the sum reaches one.  
**[34:09]** Our histogram covers one year, so the damage is a damage per year, and the fatigue life in years is simply one over that damage. The design format, formula B.3, then says that the service life must not exceed the fatigue life divided by the design fatigue factor. The same format sets inspection intervals. And table B.4 gives the factor: 10 for permanent equipment, which is assumed never to be inspected, three for the service life of temporary equipment, and 10 for inspection intervals.  
**[34:47]** For the template, Annex A sends us to NORSOK N-001, where the design fatigue factor depends on two questions. Can the detail be inspected and repaired? And would its failure have substantial consequences? A slot weld hidden behind the conductor housing cannot be inspected, and losing it would take away support from the well. So we use 10.  
**[35:13]** Now let's derive the remaining formulas of clause B.4.4. With a linear M-N curve, the damage is the sum of n i times ΔM i to the m, divided by a bar. Ask: what single moment range, applied for all n cycles, would do the same damage? Set the two damages equal. The a bar cancels, and the equivalent range is the average of ΔM to the m, raised to the power one over m. That is formula B.14. For our histogram and m equal to three, 7.47 million cycles at 683 kilonewton metres do the same damage as the whole year.  
**[36:03]** The standard goes one step further. Draw a demand curve with slope m through that equivalent point. Its intercept is log n plus m log ΔMeq. That is formula B.13. Now read that curve at 1 million cycles. The moment range there is ΔMo, formula B.12: one over m, times log a bar minus six. If we do this for our histogram with m equal to three, we get about 1335 kilonewton metres, 7 % below the 1436 in table A.7. So the tabulated single load value is slightly conservative. A nice check that we understand what it means.  
**[36:51]** Here is the payoff. If the demand and the capacity are both written at 1 million cycles, the damage of one year is just ΔMo over ΔMc, to the power m. So the fatigue life is the histogram period times ΔMc over ΔMo, to the m. Require that life divided by the design factor is at least one year, take both sides to the power one over m, and you get the screening check: ΔMc divided by D F to the one over m must be at least ΔMo. In moment space, a design factor of 10 is only a factor of 2.15, because the moment enters to the power three. The flip side: 10 % more moment costs a quarter of the life with m equal to three, and almost 40 % with m equal to five.  

## 37:57  09 Worked example

**[38:00]** Time to put it all together. Here is our example well: 350 metres of water, BOP on the wellhead, a 36 inch conductor and a 20 inch surface casing, a four slot template with 2 millimetres of clearance in the slot, soft clay, and 5 metres of cement shortfall. All inputs are illustrative teaching values, not data from a real project. The steps, though, are exactly the standard's.  
**[38:30]** Step one: 29 bins, each normalised range from table A.8 multiplied by 1436 kilonewton metres.  
**[38:41]** First, the quick screening check for the girth weld. ΔMc is 2780. Divide by 10 to the power one third, and we get 1290, which is less than 1436. The screening fails. It predicts a fatigue life of only 7.3 years, and less than one year of service. But the screening uses a single slope of three for every cycle. Most of our cycles live on the flatter slope of five, where they do far less damage. So let's do the real calculation.  
**[39:22]** For each bin, read N from the bilinear M-N curve and divide the cycles by it. Look where the damage comes from. Not from the 4 million tiny cycles, and not from the one or two monsters, but from the middle: moment ranges between about 1000 and 2300 kilonewton metres do most of the work. The damage adds up to 0.071 per year. So the fatigue life is 14 years. Divide by the design factor of 10, and the fatigue service life is 1.4 years, or about 513 days with a riser connected. More than the one year minimum. The girth weld passes.  
**[40:10]** Compare the three answers: 7.3 years from the screening, 9 years from a full sum with a single slope, and 14 years with the bilinear curve. Each step removes a layer of conservatism. Use the screening to sort designs, and do the full sum when it matters.  
**[40:31]** The template weld gets the same treatment. Its damage is 0.028 per year, a fatigue life of 36 years, and with a design factor of 10, about 3.5 years of fatigue service life. It passes too.  
**[40:49]** Now play with the slot clearance. Close the gap completely, and the template grabs a big share of the moment. The girth weld's service life jumps to more than 7 years, but the template weld drops below half a year and fails. Open the gap to 4 millimetres, and the opposite happens. Remove the template contact altogether, and the girth weld fails at just under one year. Load share moves damage around. It never deletes it. That is why clause 8.1 asks for the load share to satisfy fatigue for the wellhead system, and the template, at the same time.  
**[41:35]** Here is how the girth weld's service life responds to the other inputs. Fabrication quality is huge: an SCF of 1.2 instead of 1.1, and the weld no longer passes. Water depth is huge: in the 80 to 250 metre band the same well gets only a third of a year. And running the BOP on a christmas tree cuts the life by more than half. Soil stiffness and cement shortfall matter less for this particular weld, though they matter a lot for welds on the inner string.  
**[42:15]** So what if your well is in shallow water and fails? NORSOK points to several levers. The template can be prepared for a lateral BOP support system. If it removes 40 % of the moment, our shallow water girth weld goes from a third of a year to more than 2 years. Table J.1 lists operational levers: less LMRP overpull, a softer lower flex joint, a better riser tally, lower drill pipe tension, and avoiding beam seas. And monitoring the wellhead during operations replaces the conservative design histogram with measured loads, so the fatigue life can be updated, and extended.  

## 43:00  10 Summary

**[43:03]** Let's recap the whole method on one page. Take the annual histogram at the wellhead datum, and remember that the moment grows below it. Follow the load paths: stiffness attracts load, gaps make it nonlinear. Below the mudline the conductor is a beam on springs, with a peak moment just below the seabed. Convert moments to hot spot stresses with beam theory and stress concentration factors. Build lower bound M-N curves from a local model. Add up the damage with Palmgren Miner, divide the life by the design factor, and check it against at least one year of riser connected time.  
**[43:48]** A conductor and a template slot are among the few parts of a subsea well that have to last for the whole life of the field, and can never be inspected. The method in NORSOK U-001 lets us put a number on how much riser time they can take. Use it with care, check the S-N data against the current DNV edition, and keep monitoring.
