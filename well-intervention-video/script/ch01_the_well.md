# Ch 1: The well, and the work after it
BUDGET: 200

## 1.01 | 51 | anim
VO: First, what are we reaching into? A finished well is built from nested steel. Casing lines the hole, and cement seals the gap behind it, so nothing can leak between the rock and the well. Inside hangs the tubing, a narrower pipe that carries the oil up. Near the bottom, a packer, a plug of steel and rubber, seals the gap between tubing and casing, so the oil has only one way up. A few hundred metres below the seabed sits the downhole safety valve, held open by hydraulic pressure from the surface and built to slam shut if that pressure is lost. And on the seabed, the Christmas tree: a block of valves that controls the flow.

## 1.02 | 47 | anim
VO: Look closer at the tubing and you find it is not plain pipe. It is a toolbox, built in. Landing nipples are short sections with a machined groove inside: a seat where a plug can be locked. A sliding sleeve is a window in the tubing wall that can be opened or closed. Side-pocket mandrels are bulges in the tubing that hold small valves off to one side. And at the bottom are the perforations, the holes shot through casing and cement, where oil from the rock finally enters the well. Almost everything we do through the tubing is done to one of these, or with one of these.

Four fittings highlight in turn at their depths with a zoomed inset each: landing nipple (profile groove), sliding sleeve (window closed/open), side-pocket mandrel (off-centre pocket with a valve), perforations (tunnels through casing and cement into sand, oil arrows entering). ## 1.03 | 23 | anim
VO: Drilling builds the well. Intervention is everything we do to it afterwards: going in to measure something, or to change something. You will also hear the word workover. It is the older term, and it usually means the heavy end of the job, where the tubing itself is pulled out and replaced.
SHOT: A horizontal life-of-a-well timeline: Drill, Complete, Produce, then a loop arrow from Produce back into the well labelled "intervention" that repeats three times along the production years, then Plug and abandon. A second label "workover" sits on the heaviest loop.
FLAGS: GEN; SIM: the industry has no single formal split between "intervention" and "workover"; usage differs between companies and regions
TERMS: workover

## 1.04 | 56.5 | anim
VO: The industry sorts interventions by how much machinery they need. Light intervention goes in through the tree, on a wireline or a coiled tubing string, with a small crew, or from a small vessel. Heavy intervention uses a rig, which removes the tree, fits a stack of safety valves, and pulls the tubing out of the hole. One question separates the two: is the well live? A live well is full of pressured hydrocarbons that will flow if you open it. A killed well is filled with a heavy kill fluid that holds the pressure back, so it can be opened safely. Killing a well buys safety, but it costs days, can damage the rock, and may leave the well producing less than before. So whenever we can, we work live.

Right "HEAVY": a derrick, the tree removed, heavy fluid (amber) filling the tubing; label "killed well". A pressure bar at the top of the left well reads 200 bar; the right reads 0 with a "kill fluid holds the pressure" arrow. Then a thumb-scale: days, damage, risk piling up on the right; the left column glows.
FLAGS: GEN; SIM: classification boundaries (light, medium, heavy) differ between operators
TERMS: wireline; coiled tubing; live well; kill fluid

## 1.05 | 45 | anim
VO: Why bother? Because every producing well declines. As the reservoir is drained its pressure falls, water arrives, and the rate slides down a curve. An intervention bends that curve back up. Its value is the oil you would not otherwise have produced, minus what the job cost. And that is why the choice of method matters so much. A wire unit can be rigged up in hours by a few people. A rig is a floating industrial site that takes weeks and employs a crew many times larger. The same repair, done the cheap way, can turn a marginal job into a profitable one.
