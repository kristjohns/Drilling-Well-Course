## 00:00  Cold open

**[00:00]** 350 metres down, a drilling rig has lost its position. The riser drags the blowout preventer sideways, and the wellhead below it takes more than 12 MNm of bending, on top of the well pressure. The engineer opens the finite element model of the wellhead housing, and there it is. A red spot. 793 MPa. 50 % above the yield strength.  
**[00:30]** So, has it failed? Is the acceptance criterion the yield strength of the material? Or is it a strain limit, from an elastic plastic analysis? And what exactly should we take out of the FE model, and compare with what?  
**[00:49]** In this video we answer those questions the way NORSOK U-001, the 2026 edition, does it in its Annex B. We will read the stress strain diagram, extract stresses from an FE model, run the elastic plastic check, and at the end, give the red spot its verdict.  

## 01:10  01 Load against capacity

**[01:14]** First, which rules apply to what. Annex B is normative. It covers the pressure containing and primary structural components in the riser load path: the wellhead housing and its extension, the conductor housing and its extension, the connectors, the tree, and the workover riser equipment. Straight pipe, like the conductor itself, has its own capacity formulas in clause B.9. And the template is designed to NORSOK N-001, outside Annex B.  
**[01:47]** Annex B asks us to design against failure modes, not against a stress. The structural ones are brittle fracture, yielding, local failure, buckling and mechanical disengagement. The functional ones include hub face separation, leak tightness, galling and loss of function. Brittle fracture and environmental cracking are prevented by the material requirements, not by analysis. And the static capacity of a component is the lowest capacity over all the remaining modes.  
**[02:21]** Then comes the design format, formula B.2. The load, or load effect, shall not exceed the static design factor, Fd, times the static capacity. Fd comes from Table B.3. 0.67 for normal conditions, 0.8 for extreme, and 0.9 or 1.0 for accidental conditions. Pressure design on its own uses 0.6, and functional failure modes always use 1.0. Now look at both sides of that inequality. Both are loads. The acceptance criterion is not a stress, and not a strain. It is a load, compared with a capacity.  
**[03:08]** So the real question is: how do we find the capacity? Clause B.3.3 gives three ways. By formulas, for simple shapes like pipes and flanges. For pipe, the yield strength enters directly: the fully plastic section for seamless riser pipe, and first yield at the outer fibre for conductors and casing. Second, by elastic finite element analysis, which NORSOK limits to preliminary design. And third, by elastic plastic finite element analysis, the direct method. The yield strength enters all three, but in a different way each time, and strain only appears in the last one. To see why, we need the stress strain diagram.  

## 03:56  02 The stress-strain diagram

**[03:59]** Everything starts with a tensile test. A specimen is pulled, and we plot the force divided by the original area, against the elongation divided by the original length. First the line is straight. Stress is proportional to strain, the slope is Young's modulus, and if we unload, the specimen springs back. Then the line bends: the steel starts to yield. It hardens, up to a maximum, the tensile strength. After that the specimen necks, and finally it breaks.  
**[04:34]** Where exactly does yielding start? There is no sharp point, so we agree on one. Draw a line parallel to the elastic line, starting at 0.2 % strain. Where it crosses the curve is the yield strength. Unload from there, and the specimen keeps 0.2 % of permanent strain. NORSOK uses exactly this. The yield strength is the stress at a 0.2 % offset, and the failure mode called yielding is defined as exceeding that elastic limit of 0.2 % plastic strain.  
**[05:12]** The design does not use the result of one test. Formula B.5 takes the specified minimum yield strength, de-rated for temperature, but never more than 90 % of the de-rated minimum tensile strength. Formula B.6 gives the tensile strength. De-rating is needed above 40 °C. Our example is a wellhead housing forged in 75K material: 517 MPa minimum yield, and 655 tensile. Below 40 degrees, σy is 517, and σu is 655.  
**[05:54]** One more step. The engineering curve divides by the original area, but the specimen gets thinner. Divide by the actual area and you get the true stress, sigma times one plus epsilon, against the true strain, the natural log of one plus epsilon. The true curve keeps rising. For our steel it reaches 743 MPa at 13 % true strain. FE programs with large strains need this true curve, and if no test curve is available, NORSOK points to the material model in ASME Section VIII, Division 3, KM-620.  
**[06:36]** From the test curve, NORSOK builds three material models for the FE analysis. A straight line, for the elastic analysis. The true curve, cut off at the yield strength with no hardening, for the limit load method. A tiny slope is allowed to help the solver: no more than 5 % at 20 % plastic strain. And the true curve cut off at the true ultimate strength, for the plastic collapse method and for the local failure check. Keep these three curves in mind. Each one belongs to a different acceptance check.  

## 07:16  03 The elastic route: extracting stress

**[07:19]** Here is our wellhead housing, cut in half. The FE model divides it into small elements. At the wellhead datum we apply the accidental loads from Table A.4, plus the rated working pressure inside. A linear elastic analysis gives six stress components at every point. To plot them, we combine them into one number: the von Mises equivalent stress.  
**[07:45]** The von Mises stress takes the three principal stresses and measures how much they distort the material, rather than squeeze it. When it reaches the yield strength from the tensile test, yielding starts. NORSOK allows von Mises or Tresca for elastic analysis.  
**[08:04]** Now, the red spot. 793 MPa sits in a thin skin at the fillet, about a centimetre deep. If that skin yields, nothing collapses. The load just moves to the material next to it. A peak stress like this matters for fatigue, because that is where cracks start. For static strength, comparing the peak with the yield strength is the wrong question.  
**[08:34]** Instead, we cut through the wall with a stress classification line, from the bore to the outside, at the critical section. Along that line we read the stress components: the axial stress from bending and tension, and the hoop and radial stresses from the pressure.  
**[08:53]** Then we linearise, as described in ASME Section VIII, Division 2, Annex 5-A. The membrane stress is the average over the wall. The bending stress is the linear part with the same moment. What is left over is the peak. Do that for every component, and combine them with von Mises again. Here the membrane stress is 482 MPa, membrane plus bending is 613, and the peak adds about 180, to the total of 793.  
**[09:30]** Each part gets a category. Primary stress is what the component needs to carry the load. It does not go away when the material yields. General membrane stress is Pm, local membrane stress near a discontinuity is PL, and bending is Pb. Secondary stress, Q, comes from constraint, like thermal expansion or preload, and it relaxes once the material yields. And the peak, F, only counts for fatigue. For preloaded equipment, if you cannot separate primary from secondary, treat it all as primary.  
**[10:09]** Now we compare. Table B.7 sets the membrane stress against the yield strength, membrane plus bending against 1.5 times the yield strength, the primary plus secondary range against two times, shear against 0.57 times, and bearing against the yield strength. Why 1.5 for bending? When the wall first yields at its surface, the inside is still elastic. It can carry more, until the whole thickness has yielded. For a rectangular wall, that fully plastic moment is exactly 1.5 times the first yield moment. And 0.57 is 1/√3, the shear yield stress of von Mises.  
**[10:56]** The design factor scales the allowable stress. For the accidental load, Fd is 0.9. So the membrane stress of 482 must stay below 0.9 times 517, which is 465. It does not. The elastic route fails, by 4 %. Membrane plus bending would pass. But read the fine print. NORSOK limits elastic analysis to preliminary design, and for pressure containing parts, to a diameter to thickness ratio above 10. Our neck has 6.25. So this result cannot be used for acceptance at all. We need the direct method.  

## 11:44  04 The elastic-plastic route

**[11:47]** An elastic plastic analysis follows the material past yield. The FE model needs the von Mises yield function with associated flow, large deformations and large strains, and a hardening rule: isotropic for static capacities, kinematic if the loading reverses beyond yield. The loads are applied in the real sequence: preload, pressure and tension first. Then one load, here the bending moment, is increased step by step until the component can take no more.  
**[12:23]** First, the limit load method, with the curve cut off at the yield strength. At first the neck is elastic. At close to 90 % of the final load, the outer surface reaches 0.2 % plastic strain. It has yielded. With every step, the yielded zone grows inwards from both sides, and the rotation grows faster than the load. When the whole section has yielded, it acts as a plastic hinge. The next load step finds no equilibrium, and the solution does not converge. The last converged load is the limit load. For our neck, 20.3 MNm. That is the yielding capacity, at a design factor of one.  
**[13:11]** The second option is the factored plastic collapse method. Now the material hardens, up to the true ultimate strength. The moment climbs past the limit load and peaks at 25.2 MNm. That maximum is the plastic collapse load. It is divided by the larger of 1.2, and σu/σy. For our steel that is 1.27, which gives 19.9 MNm. Why 1.2? ASME Section VIII, Division 3, uses a margin of 1.8 on plastic collapse, and the workover riser standard uses 1.5 on yielding. 1.8 over 1.5 is 1.2, so the two methods are meant to agree. Here they do, within 2 %.  
**[14:06]** For a connector, the same curve carries more than one limit. Preload exceedance, seal leak tightness, mechanical disengagement, the limit load, and the plastic collapse load. The static capacity is the lowest of the structural and functional capacities. That is why NORSOK recommends that you always plot the load against the rotation or displacement.  
**[14:31]** Single load capacities are combined in a capacity chart. For linear behaviour, formula B.7 adds the true wall tension over the tension capacity, and the moment over the bending capacity, and the sum shall not exceed Fd. That gives one envelope per load condition: normal, extreme, and accidental. Where pressure or tension changes the bending capacity, NORSOK asks for nonlinear charts, analysed with all the loads divided by Fd.  

## 15:05  05 Where strain is the criterion

**[15:08]** So, is the acceptance criterion a strain limit? For the yielding capacity, no. It is a load, and you do not read off a strain to find it. Strain becomes the criterion in exactly two places.  
**[15:24]** The first is studs in a flange, segments in a clamp, and locking segments in a connector, when they are bent. For these parts, NORSOK does not wait for the structure to become unstable. The yielding capacity is the load at which the whole root cross section has reached 0.2 % equivalent plastic strain. A stud that has yielded right through is permanently stretched, and its preload is gone.  
**[15:54]** The second is local failure. NORSOK defines it as a ductile crack that starts at a point of high triaxial stress, as tiny voids form, grow, and join. Triaxiality is the mean stress divided by the von Mises stress. In a tensile test it is one third. At a notch, where the material is held back in every direction, it can be well above one. ASME Section VIII, Division 3, clause KD-232, gives the strain limit as an exponential function of triaxiality. At a triaxiality of 1.2, a steel has only about 18 % of the ductility it shows in a tensile test. The plastic strain is summed over the load history, preload, pressure test, and the yielding capacity, and the damage shall not exceed one. NORSOK sets the ASME load factor for this check to 1.0, and runs the history all the way up to the yielding capacity.  
**[17:00]** In practice there is a shortcut. Carbon and low alloy steels that meet the ductility requirements need no local failure check: an elongation of at least 17 % and a reduction of area of at least 35 %, for a minimum yield strength up to 655 MPa. Studs and nuts made to annex D are exempt too. Our 75K forging meets this. In the elastic route, the same idea is a simple sum: the three principal stresses together shall stay below 2.5 times the minimum yield strength.  
**[17:40]** The remaining modes are load based again. The buckling capacity is the maximum load divided by 1.10, the mechanical disengagement capacity is the load at disengagement divided by 1.1, and ratcheting only needs checking above 180 degrees. Functional capacities, like seal leak tightness and hub face separation, use an Fd of one, with acceptance criteria from the manufacturer, validated by testing.  

## 18:14  06 Worked example: the red spot

**[18:17]** Back to the red spot. Our well is in 350 metres of water, with the blowout preventer on the wellhead. Table A.4 gives the design loads at the wellhead datum. The accidental case is 12 217 kNm of bending, 892 kN of shear, and 3424 kN of tension. The neck is 0.6 metres below the datum, so the shear adds to the moment: 12.75 MNm.  
**[18:55]** The well pressure also pulls on the housing. Formula B.7 uses the true wall tension: the applied tension plus the pressure end load on the gasket sealing diameter. With 690 bar on a sealing diameter of half a metre, the end load is 13.5 MN, and the true wall tension becomes 17.0 MN.  
**[19:21]** Now the check. The limit load analysis gave a bending capacity of 20.3 MNm, and the tension capacity, σy times the area, is 107 MN. For the accidental case: 17 over 107, plus 12.75 over 20.3, divided by 0.9, gives 0.88. It passes. The extreme case, with a design factor of 0.8, gives 0.61. The same neck failed the elastic check by 4 %, because that check stops at the first yield through the wall, and ignores the reserve of the full section.  
**[20:09]** To finish the job, we tick off the other failure modes. Local failure: exempt, because the forging meets the ductility requirements. Buckling: not an issue for a wall this thick. The functional capacities of the connector, like hub face separation and gasket leak tightness at minimum preload, come from the supplier, and connector capacities from FE analysis must be validated by a full scale test. So, the verdict on the red spot: 793 MPa of peak stress, and the wellhead is still acceptable.  

## 20:48  07 Summary

**[20:51]** Let's put the answers on one page. Is the criterion the yield strength? The yield strength is the anchor of every check, but it is never compared with a peak stress. In the elastic route it limits linearised stresses, and in the elastic plastic route it shapes the material curve that produces the capacity. Is it a strain limit? Not for yielding. Strain is the criterion only for studs and segments in bending, and for local failure, unless the material is ductile enough. And from the FE model we extract linearised stresses along classification lines, or a load rotation curve to instability. Whichever route you take, the final check is always the same. The load shall not exceed Fd times the capacity.  
**[21:46]** The rules are in NORSOK U-001, Annex B. The details of the linearisation, the material curve and the strain limit are in the ASME code. The worked example used illustrative inputs, so take your design values from your own project. Thanks for watching.
